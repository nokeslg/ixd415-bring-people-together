# Loudon: 3 Interviews (Week 1)

Notes and takeaways from three recorded, in-person interviews about how people connect, what helps, and what gets in the way.

## Key takeaways across all three

1. **Real connection happens in person; online is the on-ramp.** All three said face-to-face is where friendships actually form. Online works for keeping in touch or finding out about things, not for getting close. Whatever we make should push people toward doing something together, not just looking at each other.
2. **Shared activity breaks the ice better than conversation.** A card game at a Halloween party got a room talking. Jamming with musicians at a festival felt welcoming. Swapping TikToks gives friends something to talk about. People connect more easily with a thing to do in the middle.
3. **Fear of being perceived is the big barrier for our age group.** "Nonchalant" culture, anxiety, growing up documented online, not wanting people to see what you comment on. People won't put themselves out there unless the setting makes it safe or normal to.
4. **Joining an existing group is hard unless you're clearly invited.** Jonah: it's hard to join "a pre established thing" unless "they make it clear that you're invited." Eliana and Lizzy: friendships happened when someone took the step to invite people outside of class.
5. **Niche shared interests are the glue, but they're hard to find outside school.** Clubs, bluegrass, drag, fan fiction, cats. People want a third space around a specific interest, beyond class and work friends.
6. **Signs of other people right now are appealing.** Interviewee 3 wanted AO3 to show how many people have a story open right now, not just all-time kudos, and wanted to see other players' high scores. Live presence and light competition make things feel alive.
7. **Online, people perform or get harsh.** Texting is a "sanitized," over-friendly version of yourself, with extra exclamation points and emojis. Anonymous profiles make people brazen. Being tied to who you are keeps people kind.
8. **Low effort, everyday rituals keep people close.** Daily TikTok swaps, a weekly Wednesday call with a best friend, inside jokes. Small repeated moments matter more than big events.
9. **Safety is a real concern for in-person meetups.** For a cat play date idea: meet in public first (a cat cafe), make it a club, and maybe limit who can join.

## Ideas worth exploring

- A party game where the group's own references and inside jokes are the content (from the TikTok "catch the reference" game). This could help a party game stay current instead of feeling dated.
- Small, interest-based micro events where newcomers are clearly invited, and the first meetup is in a public place.
- Live "who's here right now" signals around shared content, like a story, a song or an event.
- Bring people together with an activity, not a profile. Doing something together beats introducing yourself.

## Interview 1: Eliana and Lizzy

- **Who:** two 19-year-old KU students (nursing and journalism), freshman roommates last year. Interviewed together.
- **Feel most connected:** with friends who share interests. Eliana made most of her friends through two journalism clubs.
- **Feel most disconnected:** scrolling social media. It's "people watching," a highlight reel where you see someone's life without interacting with them.
- **Online vs. in person:** they texted before moving in as roommates but didn't really connect until living together. Texting is a nice, "sanitized" version of yourself. Online you have to be overly friendly and use extra punctuation and emojis to replace tone and body language.
- **What makes a group close:** time and shared experiences more than shared interests. Their newer nursing friends share interests but aren't as close as their old roommate.
- **Tools:** texting is the main one, and phone calls and FaceTime make Lizzy anxious. Eliana keeps a weekly Wednesday call with her best friend back home. Teams makes them more formal, like messaging a boss.
- **Good moments:** a group card game at a Halloween party got everyone talking. One of them met someone wearing the same costume, and they swapped Instagrams and still text.
- **Why it's hard for our age group:** COVID, being "nonchalant," anxiety, fear of being perceived, and growing up documented online. Older people talk to strangers because they didn't have a phone to hide in.
- **What helps:** being vulnerable and inviting people outside of class: "if you're only connecting with somebody in class, it's not really gonna go anywhere."

## Interview 2: Jonah

- **Who:** lives in Lawrence, not a student, works at a local venue. Musician and illustrator.
- **Positive recent experience:** playing music with people at the Winfield Bluegrass Festival. Experienced players supported beginners.
- **Online:** doesn't connect well in digital spaces besides YouTube, mostly traditional music. Small niche communities are easier to find in person.
- **Where people are nicer:** in person. Anonymous profiles make people brazen. In person, you're responsible for what you say.
- **Barriers in person:** people his age stay in their own groups and feel skeptical and awkward with strangers. People in their 40s and 50s are friendlier because they grew up before the internet.
- **Social life:** wishes he hung out more, but it's hard to find common ground. People used to be pushed into the same spaces; now they have to seek them out, and joining a group that already exists is hard unless you're clearly invited.
- **Bigger picture:** thinks this is likely the loneliest generation, even though we can reach more people than ever. Talked about empathy and getting outside yourself.
- **What bridged the gap:** traveling. A small Latin American music festival in a Welsh town of about 2,000 people showed how a community can make newcomers feel cared for.
- **Wishes:** more third spaces, and an internet that helps real-life events happen instead of replacing them. People who don't use social media miss out on events.

## Interview 3: TikTok, AO3 and games

- **Who:** a friend of Loudon's. Recently went to drag shows several times.
- **Drag shows:** fun and engaging. Being packed into a room gets you talking to the people next to you, but you're mostly there to watch. Interactive events like drag story times or arts and crafts are more of a "do together" thing.
- **TikTok:** sends TikToks with a friend every day. They turn into inside jokes and a game of who catches which reference, and they fill silences in real conversations. Reads comments all the time but doesn't comment, because people can see what you watch. Comments are a big part of the experience: they point out details you missed and spark debate.
- **AO3 (fan fiction):** very community-driven. Tags help find a niche, and the etiquette is to leave kudos and comments because writers do it for free. Writers reply, which feels rewarding. Recommends stories with a friend. The community feels most real when people talk about it on TikTok and you can see faces.
- **What AO3 is missing:** messaging, discussion boards, and a live signal like "how many people have this open right now."
- **Genshin Impact:** co-op lets up to 4 players fight together, and you see their icon and name. No voice chat, probably for safety. Typing mid-fight on an iPad is clunky, and the emotes help a little. Wishes they could play the story together.
- **Idea of their own:** a cat play date community. To make it safe: meet first at a cat cafe, make it a club, maybe women only. Valued as a third space away from class and work friends.
- **Crashy Cats:** plays solo with no leaderboard. Wants to see everyone's high scores, and thinks it would be fun with a second player.
